Evaluating compression

TL;DR: Compression ratio means nothing without a quality metric, and the metric you pick can change which methods look safe.

Compression ratio is not a metric

A method that compresses to 10% of the original cache but erases vital information is worse than one that compresses to 50% while preserving it. Chasing compression ratio alone misses that tradeoff. The metrics below are what actually measure it, each with its own strengths and weaknesses, which is why the field doesn't settle on just one.

Perplexity

Perplexity asks a narrow question: given everything before this point, how surprised is the model by the actual next token? Average that over a held-out text and you have the metric. It's essentially free, since it needs no labeled answers and no task-specific harness, just a forward pass and a formula. That's why it's the metric that shows up first in almost every compression paper's ablations.

The narrowness is also the weakness. Next-token prediction depends mostly on local, syntactic context. The word after "the capital of France is" is easy to guess even if the model has forgotten a fact three thousand tokens back that a real question would have needed. A method can evict exactly the token a user's query would have needed, and perplexity on generic held-out text often won't move enough to notice. It's a decent early smoke test for "did this method break the model outright," and a poor proxy for "can this model still use its long context."

Needle-in-a-haystack

Needle-in-a-haystack, popularized by Greg Kamradt's testing framework, is the direct fix for perplexity's blind spot. Insert one specific fact (the "needle") at a controlled position inside a long, otherwise irrelevant document (the "haystack"), then ask the model to retrieve it after compression. Sweep the needle's depth and the context length together and the result is a heatmap of exactly where retrieval starts failing, including the well-known failure mode where facts buried in the middle of a long context are recalled far worse than ones near the start or end.

It's precise about the failure it's designed to catch, since a method that evicts the needle's tokens fails hard and visibly. What it doesn't capture is anything more diffuse. A method that keeps every single needle perfectly while quietly degrading the model's ability to synthesize several facts together, or to follow an argument that depends on tone and structure rather than one retrievable sentence, can look flawless on this test and still be much worse to actually use.

RULER and LongBench

Broader long-context suites exist precisely to catch what a single needle can't. RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024) keeps the synthetic, controllable spirit of needle-in-a-haystack but expands it, adding multiple needles at once, distractor needles designed to confuse retrieval, and non-retrieval task types like multi-hop tracing and aggregation across the whole context, all at systematically varied lengths. Its headline result was itself a finding worth noting: of the models tested that advertised context windows of 32K tokens or more, only about half actually held up once evaluated at that length; the rest degraded well before reaching what they claimed to support.

LongBench (Bai et al., 2023) takes a different angle, favoring realistic tasks over synthetic ones. It spans 21 datasets across six task categories (single- and multi-document QA, summarization, few-shot learning, synthetic tasks, and code completion) in both English and Chinese, with documents averaging several thousand words. A method has to hold up across summarization and code and multi-hop QA simultaneously, which is a much harder bar to clear by accident than one retrievable fact.

Neither suite is strictly "better" than needle-in-a-haystack. They answer a broader question at a higher compute cost per method evaluated. That tradeoff is exactly why perplexity, a single needle sweep, and a full suite all still coexist: cheap-and-narrow for fast iteration, expensive-and-broad for the number you'd actually trust.

Where this leaves you

A compression ratio by itself is a cost, not a result. Reading one without a paired quality number is the single most common way this catalogue's methods get compared unfairly. The paired quality number needs to be read alongside which kind of metric produced it: perplexity is the cheapest and the most likely to miss a real problem; needle-in-a-haystack catches the specific failure of "dropped the fact that mattered" but not diffuse degradation; RULER and LongBench cost more to run per method but are the hardest to accidentally pass. A method that only reports perplexity hasn't shown you very much. A method with numbers on all three has actually earned its compression ratio.
