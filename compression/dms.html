compression / threshold-based

DMS: Dynamic Memory Sparsification

TL;DR: Every other method in this catalogue picks a compression ratio up front and works out which tokens meet it. DMS flips that around: you pick a score threshold, and however many tokens clear it is however many survive. The actual compression ratio is a result, not an input, and it changes with the content.

A threshold, not a target

kvpress's DMSPress wraps any other scorer press in this catalogue, SnapKV, Knorm, TOVA, whatever you'd otherwise use, and changes how its scores get turned into an eviction decision. Instead of ranking every token and keeping the top N × (1 − ratio) of them, it evicts anything scoring below a threshold and keeps everything else, no matter how many tokens that turns out to be. A sliding_window_size (128 by default) always protects the most recent tokens: scores accumulate in a buffer as tokens arrive, and only once the buffer grows past that window does the oldest overflow actually get compared against the threshold and possibly evicted.

Because the amount of compression depends on how many scores clear the bar, and that depends on the content, compression_ratio isn't something you set on this press. It's a read-only property, computed from how much actually got evicted after a forward pass; the source raises an AttributeError if you try to assign it directly. That's a deliberate design choice, not an oversight: a fixed ratio and a fixed threshold are two different contracts, and DMSPress only offers the second one.

DMSPress can also run during decoding, not just prefill, if decoding=True: the score buffer keeps accumulating and evicting as generation continues, which most fixed-ratio presses in this catalogue simply can't do, their budget is locked in once at prefill.

What the real DMS paper does that this press doesn't

The name comes from Łańcucki, Staniszewski, Nawrot, and Ponti's "Inference-Time Hyper-Scaling with KV Cache Compression" (arXiv 2506.05345). Their DMS trains a dedicated eviction mechanism, in as little as 1,000 training steps, that postpones eviction to merge token representations rather than discarding them outright, reaching up to 8x compression while preserving or improving accuracy. The freed-up budget is then spent generating more tokens within the same compute envelope, which is where "hyper-scaling" comes from.

kvpress is direct about the gap here: its own docstring states that DMSPress implements a dense-prefill version of the idea, not the sparse-prefill version, and does not include the trained evictor modules from the paper. What you get in kvpress is the threshold-instead-of-ratio interface applied generically to any existing scorer, not a reproduction of the paper's trained merging pipeline. For something closer to the actual paper, the docstring points to nvidia/Qwen3-8B-DMS-8x on Hugging Face and NVIDIA/Model-Optimizer's experimental/dms on GitHub.

Try it

This isn't a compression-ratio slider like the other pages: DMS's whole point is that the ratio isn't chosen, it falls out of the threshold and the content. Move the threshold and watch the kept count shift; then switch the sample variant at the same threshold and watch the kept count shift again, purely because the (simulated) underlying scores are different, not because any setting changed.

Tokens with a colored border are inside the sliding window and are always kept, they're never even compared against the threshold. Everything else survives only if its score clears the bar.

Simulated, not measured. The per-token scores driving this demo are a deterministic stand-in for whatever underlying scorer press you'd actually wrap DMSPress around; there's no real forward pass or trained evictor behind them. The "sample variant" control exists only to show that a fixed threshold produces a different kept count on different content, which is the mechanism's entire point.

Where this shines, and where it doesn't

A threshold adapts to content automatically: a context that's mostly redundant compresses hard at a fixed decision rule, one that's mostly novel information barely compresses at all, without anyone having to guess the right ratio ahead of time. It can also keep sparsifying through decoding, not just once at prefill, and it composes with whichever scorer press you'd otherwise reach for in this catalogue, DMSPress is a wrapper, not a scoring method of its own.

The costs are the mirror image of that flexibility. A threshold has no universal meaning by itself, it's calibrated to whatever the wrapped scorer's score() actually outputs, a raw dot product, a z-score, a negated L2 norm, and a value tuned for one scorer or model is meaningless for another; it has to be re-tuned. The total memory footprint also stops being predictable ahead of time the way a ratio-based cache's is, which matters if you need a hard memory ceiling rather than a best-effort one. And as covered above, kvpress's own docstring is explicit that this implementation approximates DMS's threshold-based interface far more than it reproduces the paper's actual sparse-prefill, trained-evictor mechanism.

Real implementation

kvpress implements this as DMSPress, which wraps a press: ScorerPress and evicts scores below threshold, subject to sliding_window_size. See the source: dms_press.py. Original paper: Łańcucki, Staniszewski, Nawrot, Ponti, "Inference-Time Hyper-Scaling with KV Cache Compression," 2025.
