compression / norm-based

CUR

TL;DR: Knorm scores a token by its key vector's own length. CUR (kvpress calls the underlying method CurDKV) scores it by both its key and value vector's length, multiplied together, and judges that magnitude against a small local neighborhood of nearby tokens rather than the whole cached sequence.

Leverage scores, borrowed from matrix decomposition

Sengupta, Chaudhary, and Chakraborty motivate this from CUR matrix decomposition: a classic technique for approximating a large matrix by keeping a subset of its actual rows and columns rather than some abstract low-rank combination of them. Which rows and columns to keep is decided by leverage scores, a measure of how much each row or column contributes to reconstructing the original matrix, and one cheap, well-known approximation to a row's leverage score is simply its own squared magnitude relative to the others. Applied to a KV cache, that means: a cached token's key and value vectors are rows of the (growing) key and value matrices, and their squared norms approximate how much removing that row would actually hurt reconstructing those matrices.

In code this is nothing more exotic than two sums of squares. kvpress's CURPress.score() computes k2 = (keys**2).sum(dim=-1) and v2 = (values**2).sum(dim=-1), literally each token's squared key-vector and value-vector L2 norm. (There's also an optional use_random_leverage mode, off by default, that first projects keys and values through a random Gaussian matrix before squaring: a cheaper, randomized leverage-score estimate, common in the CUR-decomposition literature, that this demo doesn't expose.)

Worth flagging directly: this scores in the opposite direction from Knorm. Knorm evicts high-norm keys because Devoto et al. found, empirically, that low-norm keys receive more future attention. CUR's default keeps high-norm keys and values, because its leverage-score framing targets a different objective: approximating the K/V matrices themselves as well as possible, not predicting attention weights. That's not a contradiction so much as two methods optimizing for different things; which one produces a better cache in practice is an empirical question the CUR paper answers on its own benchmark suite, not something derivable from the formulas alone.

Judged locally, not globally

By default (use_local_approximation=True), CUR doesn't compare a token's magnitude against the entire cached sequence. It splits the sequence into non-overlapping windows of local_window_size tokens (16 by default) and renormalizes k2 and v2 to sum to 1 within each window before combining them. A token's score is really "how large is this token relative to just its own nearby neighbors," not "relative to the whole prompt." The two per-token signals are then combined by leverage_type, default "kv_product": an elementwise product, so a token needs to be locally salient in both key and value magnitude to score highly (the alternatives, "kv_avg", "key", and "value", aren't exposed in the demo below). Scores are then renormalized once more across the whole sequence, and the first num_sinks tokens (4 by default) are forced to the top of the range, so they're the last thing cut when the budget gets tight, similar to the sink-token treatment StreamingLLM uses (though StreamingLLM's sink is unconditional, not just rank-boosted).

Where this shines, and where it doesn't

Like Knorm, CUR's scoring is cheap: it needs nothing beyond the key and value vectors that already exist as a byproduct of the model's forward pass, no attention matrix or observation window required. The paper reports up to 9.6% higher accuracy than SnapKV and ChunkKV on its evaluation, and up to a 40% generation-latency reduction at high compression ratios. The local-window design is also a deliberate tradeoff: judging a token against only its immediate neighbors means a genuinely unique, isolated token can still lose to a merely-large neighbor within its own window even if it would have ranked well globally, and the window size itself becomes another hyperparameter to tune per model or task, on top of the compression ratio.
