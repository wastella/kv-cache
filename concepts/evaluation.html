concepts / methodology

Evaluating compression

Every method in the catalogue can report a compression ratio — remove half the cache, remove 90% of it, the number is trivial to produce. None of that number tells you whether the model still works. "Good" only means something once you attach a quality metric to the ratio, and — this is the part that trips people up — different metrics disagree with each other about which methods are safe.

Compression ratio is not a metric

A method that compresses to 10% of the original cache but silently drops the one sentence a downstream question depends on is worse than one that only reaches 40% but keeps it. Ratio measures cost. It says nothing about what broke. Every number below is a way of measuring what broke, and each one is blind to a different kind of failure — which is why the field doesn't settle on just one.

Perplexity: cheap, and mostly blind at long range

Perplexity asks a narrow question — given everything before this point, how surprised is the model by the actual next token? — and averages that over a held-out text. It's essentially free: no labeled answers, no task-specific harness, just a forward pass and a formula, so it's the metric that shows up first in almost every compression paper's ablations.

The narrowness is also the weakness. Next-token prediction mostly depends on local, syntactic context — the next word after "the capital of France is" is easy to guess well even if the model has forgotten a fact three thousand tokens back that a real question would have needed. A method can evict exactly the token a user's query would have needed, and perplexity on generic held-out text often won't move enough to notice. It's a decent early smoke test for "did this method break the model outright," and a poor proxy for "can this model still use its long context."

Needle-in-a-haystack: did it keep the one sentence that mattered

Needle-in-a-haystack, popularized by Greg Kamradt's testing framework, is the direct fix for perplexity's blind spot: insert one specific fact (the "needle") at a controlled position inside a long, otherwise irrelevant document (the "haystack"), then ask the model to retrieve it after compression. Sweep the needle's depth and the context length together and you get a heatmap of exactly where retrieval starts failing — including the well-known failure mode where facts buried in the middle of a long context are recalled far worse than ones near the start or end.

It's precise about the failure it's designed to catch: a method that evicts the needle's tokens fails hard and visibly. What it doesn't capture is anything more diffuse — a method that keeps every single needle perfectly while quietly degrading the model's ability to synthesize several facts together, or to follow an argument that depends on tone and structure rather than one retrievable sentence, can look flawless on this test and still be much worse to actually use.

RULER and LongBench: harder to game than a single needle

Broader long-context suites exist precisely to catch what a single needle can't. RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024) keeps the synthetic, controllable spirit of needle-in-a-haystack but expands it — multiple needles at once, distractor needles designed to confuse retrieval, and non-retrieval task types like multi-hop tracing and aggregation across the whole context, all at systematically varied lengths. Its headline result was itself an evaluation finding: of the models tested that advertise context windows of 32K tokens or more, only about half actually maintained satisfactory performance once evaluated at 32K — the rest degraded well before reaching the length they claimed to support.

LongBench (Bai et al., 2023) takes a different angle — realistic tasks instead of synthetic ones: 21 datasets across six task categories (single- and multi-document QA, summarization, few-shot learning, synthetic tasks, and code completion) in both English and Chinese, with documents averaging several thousand words. A method has to hold up across summarization and code and multi-hop QA simultaneously, which is a much harder bar to clear by accident than one retrievable fact.

Neither suite is strictly "better" than needle-in-a-haystack — they answer a broader question at higher compute cost per method evaluated, which is exactly why perplexity, a single needle sweep, and a full suite all still coexist: cheap-and-narrow for fast iteration, expensive-and-broad for the number you'd actually trust.

Where this leaves you

A compression ratio by itself is a cost, not a result — reading one without a paired quality number is the single most common way this catalogue's methods get compared unfairly. The paired quality number needs to be read alongside which kind of metric produced it: perplexity is the cheapest and the most likely to miss a real problem; needle-in-a-haystack catches the specific failure of "dropped the fact that mattered" but not diffuse degradation; RULER and LongBench cost more to run per method but are the hardest to accidentally pass. A method that only reports perplexity hasn't shown you very much. A method with numbers on all three has actually earned its compression ratio.

Real implementation

kvpress ships its own evaluation/ module — a CLI that runs a given press against long-context datasets including RULER, LongBench, LongBench-v2, Loogle, InfiniteBench, ZeroSCROLLS, and needle-in-a-haystack, and saves predictions plus computed metrics per run. See the source: evaluation/ in the kvpress repo. Real, measured results for the catalogue's methods live on kvpress's own leaderboard: kvpress-leaderboard on Hugging Face.
